# Editing Standard

Use these rule IDs in the revision table. Apply only rules relevant to the passage.

## Accuracy and Meaning

- **G1 — Mechanical accuracy:** Check spelling, typography, punctuation, missing or duplicated words, agreement, tense, articles, units, symbols, figure/table callouts, and malformed characters sentence by sentence. Then inspect every substantive phrase for grammar, natural collocation, and exact meaning; an understandable sentence can still contain an unacceptable local construction.
- **G2 — Preserve meaning and stance:** Do not change the study design, direction or magnitude of an effect, biological entity, comparison, time point, dose, sample size, statistical result, or the author's intended rhetorical strength without evidence and author confirmation. Do not systematically downgrade claims merely because a more cautious formulation is possible.
- **G3 — Evidence strength without defensive prose:** Match language to the design. Association does not establish causation; expression data alone do not establish pathway activation, function, therapeutic efficacy, or mechanism. When a claim is arguable and supported by the overall evidence chain, preserve its strength. Do not routinely add a conservative alternative; provide claim-calibration options only when the user requests them or when ambiguity materially affects scientific accuracy. Revise the passage itself only for a clear design-to-claim error.
- **G4 — Concrete scientific detail:** Replace vague timing, population, or setting language with exact information only when supplied or supported by a source. Otherwise flag the missing detail; never fabricate specificity.
- **G4a — Missing values and placeholders:** Never invent statistical support, sample sizes, scale-bar lengths, supplementary figure numbers, reference numbers, or other missing values. If a direction is visible or supplied but the test is missing, write a direct descriptive result and flag the missing statistic outside the passage. Keep every unresolved value as an explicit placeholder in the delivered text and list it among outstanding items; never estimate, silently omit, round, reconstruct, or promote a placeholder to a value.

## Logic and Readability

- **G5 — Result-first information focus:** First identify the one result the reader should remember. Put the biological entity, comparison, or main finding in the grammatical subject or opening clause when this produces natural English. Avoid `analysis showed`, `we performed`, and `to determine` when the result can begin directly, but do not force an awkward passive construction merely to demote the method.
- **G5a — Figure-level logic before sentence-level style:** In multi-panel Results, determine the overall figure question, the role of each panel, and the dependency between adjacent panels before rewriting individual sentences. Result-first style must not turn panels into disconnected observations.
- **G5b — Purposeful bridges:** Preserve or add one concise bridge when a new panel tests a property not established by the previous panel, such as specificity, independence, mechanism, persistence, mediation, or external validity. The bridge should name the unresolved question, not merely announce another analysis.
- **G5c — Minimal bridge wording:** Name only the highest-level unresolved property needed to orient the reader. Do not restate its logical opposite or enumerate alternatives already evident from the comparison groups.
- **G5d — Focus without grammatical distortion:** Choose sentence structure by information focus, not by a blanket ban on method-led syntax. Use `identified`, `selected`, `derived`, or `retained` according to what the model actually did; do not use `discovered` merely to sound more innovative, and do not force an awkward passive construction solely to move the method away from subject position.
- **G6 — English logic:** Make parallel, sequential, causal, explanatory, inferential, and contrastive relations explicit where needed. Prefer a defensible chain of evidence over a list of loosely related observations. Remove redundant transitions and repeated conclusions.
- **G6a — Connector logic:** Treat connectors as statements about the relationship between propositions. Use `thus` or `therefore` only when the preceding evidence directly supports or logically yields the following conclusion; never add them as generic markers of brevity or emphasis. Use `These findings indicate` for a direct evidence-to-interpretation step, `These findings suggest` for a plausible but non-exclusive interpretation, `consistently` for concordant independent evidence, and `may explain` when a later result offers a reason for an earlier finding. Use no connector when sentence order already makes the relation clear.
- **G6b — Positive scientific motivation:** In Results, motivate the next analysis through the scientific opportunity created by the preceding result or the additional capability of the new design. Do not write bridges led by `because the previous dataset lacked`, `owing to insufficient data`, `to overcome the limitation`, or equivalent deficit framing. Report necessary limitations separately in the appropriate Discussion, Methods, or Editorial notes; never use them as the narrative engine of Results.
- **G6c — Evidence-derived bridge:** A transition should connect two evidence nodes, not merely announce chronology. Ask what the preceding finding made scientifically important to test next, then state only the premise needed to make the following analysis intelligible.
- **G6d — Framework before wording:** Before rephrasing a sentence, verify that both sides of a contrast are parallel, listed items belong to the same category, every comparison has an explicit referent, and no stated count is already entailed by another clause. Rebuild the sentence when its framework fails; do not polish inside a defective structure.
- **G7 — Concision:** Preserve information while shortening long noun strings, repeated qualifiers, duplicate methods, redundant transitions, and repeated conclusions. On polishing tasks, perform a final compression pass and prefer the shortest conventional sentence that preserves the intended meaning and emphasis. Split or merge according to logic, not a fixed length rule.
- **G7a — One-sentence sufficiency:** If one sentence can communicate the result and its necessary inference, use one sentence. Do not distribute one point across a purpose sentence, a result sentence, a paraphrased interpretation, and a concluding restatement. Every additional sentence must contribute new evidence, a necessary contrast, or a distinct inference.
- **G7b — No delayed result:** Put the central entity, comparison, or quantitative result before secondary procedural detail. Remove generic scene-setting such as `to further explore` or retrospective experiment summaries only when panel order and the preceding result already make the purpose clear. Retain a brief `To determine whether...` or equivalent bridge when it supplies the otherwise missing reason for the next analysis.
- **G7c — No same-level semantic echo:** Delete paragraph-ending formulations such as `These findings indicate`, `Taken together`, or `This result is consistent with` only when they repeat the preceding observation at the same conceptual level. Keep a summary that converts numbers into the central innovation, comparative advantage, mechanism, or explanation.
- **G7d — Selective detail:** Results should carry the data needed for the main argument, not reproduce every number visible in a figure or table. Preserve decisive effect sizes, uncertainty, significance, and comparisons; compress secondary values and routine methods when their omission does not change interpretation.
- **G7e — Logic-preserving compression:** Delete lexical repetition, duplicated interpretation, and routine procedural detail before deleting motivation or transitions. Compression is successful only when the revised passage is shorter and the panel-to-panel argument remains fully recoverable.
- **G7f — No orphan analyses:** If a reader can understand what an analysis found but not why it was performed, the prose is under-explained. Add the shortest scientifically meaningful bridge linking the prior finding to the new question.
- **G7g — No entailed repetition:** Delete a statement when another statement in the same sentence necessarily conveys it. A superlative already places the alternatives below it, a peak entails a later decline, and a sole exception entails that the remaining items are not exceptions. Report the implied detail only when its exact value carries separate analytical importance.
- **G7h — Answer-bearing transition:** When a panel explains a preceding pattern, combine the bridge, analytical basis, and answer where possible without coining a label for that pattern. Avoid a separate question sentence that previews the same categories reported in the conclusion.
- **G7i — Semantic-lift test:** Before deleting a summary sentence, compare its function with the preceding sentence. If the preceding sentence reports observations and the summary states why they matter to the paper, the summary adds a semantic level and should remain.
- **G7j — Evidence-module closure:** Identify distinct evidence modules within a figure and give each central module its own concise closure. Example: panels showing incremental case capture support the marker's comparative advantage; a subsequent survival panel supports long-term prognostic relevance. Do not treat the second conclusion as a replacement for the first.
- **G7k — Cross-module explanation:** If the second module can explain the first, add a separate synthesis sentence rather than forcing the relationship to remain implicit. Preserve an author's clear integrative term when it accurately communicates the combined implication; do not automatically dilute it into another recital of the measurements.
- **G7l — Prefer simpler clause structure:** Prefer a phrase or a separate short sentence to a postposed `which` or `that` clause when meaning is preserved. Common reductions include `, which showed X` to `, showing X` or `, with X`, and `, which led to X` to `, leading to X`. Keep the clause when it carries information of equal weight or requires its own tense.
- **G7m — No repeated designation or stacked near-synonyms:** Within one sentence, do not name the same object twice, pair near-synonyms for the same concept, or state the same time qualifier twice. Use an unambiguous pronoun or shorter reference where possible.
- **G8 — Clear reference:** Ensure `it`, `they`, `this`, `these`, and similar expressions have one unmistakable antecedent. Prefer the entity name when ambiguity is possible.
- **G9 — Sentence variety with precision:** Vary structure when repetition distracts, including overuse of `suggesting that`, `indicating that`, purpose infinitives, or the same transition. Do not force synonyms, compressed labels, or unusual collocations merely to avoid repetition. A purpose infinitive is legitimate and often clearest; the problem is consecutive use, several instances in one paragraph, or use at the opening of most paragraphs in a section.
- **G9a — Purpose-clause variation:** When a purpose opener repeats, revise only the purpose clause and preserve the approved remainder of the sentence. Rotate to a genuinely different grammatical category rather than a synonymous opener, and consult the substitution library under R17 in [section-guides.md](section-guides.md). Do not remove purpose information that the evidence sequence needs.

## Natural Biomedical Style

- **G10 — Plain professional wording:** Prefer familiar, precise verbs and concrete observations. Be cautious with dense use of terms such as `elucidate`, `unravel`, `shed light on`, `underscore`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `intricate`, `multifaceted`, `robust`, `notably`, `remarkably`, `collectively`, `novel insights`, `underlying mechanism`, and `potential therapeutic target`.
- These expressions are not forbidden. Retain one when it is conventional, accurate, and proportionate to the evidence. The goal is not mechanical synonym substitution.
- Delete empty evaluative openers such as `Interestingly` or `Importantly` when the result itself carries the emphasis.
- Prefer specific descriptions (`was higher`, exact effect and *P* value) over unqualified `significantly`, `markedly`, or `substantially`. Use `significant` only for statistical significance unless context clearly indicates otherwise.
- Avoid repetitive connector templates. Often the logical order of sentences makes `furthermore`, `moreover`, `therefore`, or `taken together` unnecessary.
- Do not add reviewer-facing limitations or self-protective qualifications to Results as stylistic filler. Keep them in Editorial notes unless they are necessary to prevent a false statement.
- **G10a — Direction before abstraction:** When the result has a known direction, name it directly. Prefer `remained elevated`, `decreased`, or `returned toward baseline` to `remained abnormal` or an invented state label. Use `state`, `signal`, `profile`, `landscape`, `coverage`, or `alteration` only when the noun has a clear referent and adds a useful level of interpretation.
- **G10b — Qualifier necessity test:** Retain `clinical`, `cohort-level`, `individual-level`, `persistent`, and similar qualifiers only when they distinguish one state, analytical level, or time frame from another. Remove a qualifier when the surrounding comparison already supplies that distinction.
- **G10c — Time-course clarity:** State the time window, direction, and event relation separately enough to remain interpretable. Use `throughout` only when the pattern persists across the full interval; otherwise name the actual portion of the interval supported by the data.
- **G10d — Strongest defensible wording:** Evaluate titles and take-home sentences against the complete evidence chain, not one panel in isolation, and use the strongest scientifically defensible formulation. Preserve supported terms such as `specific`, `superior`, `most prominent`, `persistent`, `improved`, `drives`, or `mediates`; do not replace them with flatter descriptions merely because a more conservative alternative exists. Downgrade only for a clear evidence-to-claim mismatch. Before substituting a stronger verb, verify that the subject-verb pairing has a real literature precedent; when the subject has a small literature base, prefer a conventional noun-based assertion to an unprecedented strong-verb collocation, and record the reason for a replacement.
- **G10e — Title grammar and force:** A strong title must still use the correct scientific noun and natural syntax. Preserve a supported comparative claim while using the scientific noun appropriate to the measured domain.
- **G10f — Author revisions reveal intent, not automatic copy:** Treat the author's edits and explicitly approved wording as anchors for the desired focus, logic, and rhetorical strength. Revise only the defective portion unless the approved wording itself requires change; propose that change before making it. Still correct local grammar, agreement, articles, and collocation, and verify uncertain source-language pairings before translating them.

## Terminology and Nomenclature

- **G11 — Verified terminology:** Confirm specialized names, diseases, assays, cell types, drugs, biomarkers, and preferred field expressions using authoritative nomenclature sources, reporting guidelines, the target journal, or strong recent papers. Provide the supporting link or citation; do not claim verification without a source.
- **G11a — Verified collocations:** Check uncertain verb-object and subject-verb pairings against real biomedical usage rather than intuition. Use exact-phrase counts only as screening, then read the source sentences to confirm the subject type, syntactic role, and evidentiary strength. Treat zero or single-digit results as a reason to rephrase, not as proof of novelty. For the full procedure and search limitations, read [corpus-and-style-audit.md](corpus-and-style-audit.md).
- **G11b — Manuscript-wide terminology consistency:** Choose one designation for each procedure, treatment protocol, cohort, and model and use it across the main text, Methods, and legends. Record the choice when more than one field-accepted term exists. Match wording to the measurement domain: name proteins as proteins and transcripts as transcripts rather than merging different biological levels into one term.
- **G12 — Symbol typography:** Apply current official nomenclature for the stated species and verify each symbol. Italicize a symbol only when it denotes a gene or transcript; set the corresponding protein upright. Human gene symbols are generally uppercase, whereas mouse and other non-human gene symbols generally use an initial capital; follow the relevant authority and the target journal. Keep protein names, statistical symbols, and figure-axis labels upright. Name the measured biological level explicitly and verify cross-species symbol correspondence rather than assuming the same string.
- **G13 — Abbreviation units:** Apply the independent-unit model listed in `SKILL.md`. Define an abbreviation at first use within each unit, use it consistently thereafter, and do not introduce one that is never reused. A figure legend's terminal abbreviation list covers the artwork as well as the prose. Do not define universally recognized units or symbols unnecessarily, and follow a target journal's documented exception.
- **G14 — Passive versus single-verb alternatives:** When an idiomatic single verb could replace `be` plus a past participle, provide both the retained passive and the single-verb alternative when the choice affects tone or meaning, explain the trade-off, and let the author decide. Recommend the passive when the agent is unknown or irrelevant, the observed state is the focus, or the passive is a fixed collocation. Do not assume the single verb is automatically better.
- **G15 — Independent-clause limit:** Unless the user or target journal requests otherwise, split two or more independent subject-predicate pairs joined by `and`, `but`, `so`, or a semicolon into separate sentences. Subordinate clauses and coordinate phrases that share one subject are unaffected.
- **G16 — No noun stacking before the predicate:** Keep the grammatical subject close to its predicate. Do not place two or more modifying nouns between the head of the subject and its verb when a shorter noun phrase or a later modifier preserves the meaning.
- **G17 — Three-layer group naming:** Keep artwork labels, descriptive prose or legend names, and the model name distinct. Use one fixed short label inside figures; define the descriptive full name and short label at first prose use; use the model name for the model itself but never as a group label. State the mapping once where the design is introduced, and create a separate naming layer when study arms differ along more than one design dimension.

## Citation Check

- Mark externally verifiable background, prevalence, threshold, mechanism, guideline, and prior-work claims that lack support.
- Do not require references for the authors' own reported results, self-evident transitions, or ordinary methods details unless attribution is needed.
- Recommend up to three directly relevant references per sentence. Prefer primary studies for specific findings and authoritative guidelines or high-quality reviews for broad definitions.
- Never preserve a citation merely because it is present: verify that it supports the exact claim when citation checking is requested or when the mismatch is apparent.
