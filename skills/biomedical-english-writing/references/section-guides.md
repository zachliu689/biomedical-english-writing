# Section Guides

Read only the section needed for the current task.

## Abstract — A

Required input: objective, design or key methods, rough ordered results, principal conclusion, and target journal format or word limit when available.

- **A1:** Open with concise context, then a clear unresolved problem or gap. A contrast such as `However` is useful only when it expresses a real turn.
- **A2:** Summarize the complete study design at high level and report only the first layer of major results. Keep methods brief but do not omit a design element needed to interpret the findings.
- **A3:** Organize results in the same causal or evidentiary order as the paper. Avoid `we` except where journal style or the final conclusion makes it natural.
- **A4:** Use past tense for performed work and observed results. Use present tense only for accepted knowledge or a conclusion whose current validity is intentionally asserted. Do not impose one tense mechanically if the target journal differs.
- **A5:** End with the evidence-supported conclusion and a specific significance statement. Do not promote an untested extension to a therapeutic claim.
- **A6:** Abstracts normally contain no citations; still verify factual and terminology accuracy during drafting.

## Introduction — I

Required input: rough results, study objective, key design innovations, field, target journal, and existing references.

- **I1:** Use a funnel: clinical or biological context → focused recent knowledge → unresolved question → motivation and approach.
- **I2:** Derive the gap from what the study design can address. Do not announce the discovered molecule or result as the gap merely to manufacture novelty.
- **I3:** Explain why the unanswered question is worth resolving. Motivation may arise from a new method, population, model, temporal design, spatial resolution, or other meaningful capability—not only a defect in prior work.
- **I4:** The final paragraph should connect objective, key methods, ordered major findings, and the question addressed. Use past tense for methods performed and results observed, while allowing present tense for the current aim or a conclusion when scientifically appropriate. Keep it consistent with the abstract, results, and first/last discussion paragraphs.
- **I5:** Search and verify the literature for background and gap claims. Present the knowledge landscape fairly, including evidence that complicates the proposed narrative.

## Results — R

Required input per turn: one figure, its panel labels, underlying comparison/conditions, sample sizes and replicate definitions, statistics, and the corresponding draft or result notes.

- **R1:** Inspect the figure before editing or drafting. Describe only visible or supplied results; do not infer missing measurements.
- **R1a — Figure argument map:** Before writing, identify the figure's overall question and assign each panel an argumentative role, such as cohort definition, discovery, feature selection, specificity testing, mechanistic decomposition, temporal characterization, intervention, or validation. Record what result from each panel motivates the next panel. Do not treat a multi-panel figure as a list of independent outputs.
- **R2:** Lead with the biological result or comparison, not the analysis procedure. Use the strongest concise inference supported by the supplied evidence chain; Results may advance the paper's mechanism rather than merely catalogue observations.
- **R2a — Orienting opening:** The first paragraph for a figure must briefly establish the scientific problem and the design needed to interpret the first result. Result-first writing does not require an abrupt opening that leaves the cohort, comparison, or purpose unexplained.
- **R2b — Natural result emphasis:** Result-first writing concerns information priority, not a mandatory grammatical template. Put the result first when natural, but do not create passive or nominalized wording such as `was further discovered among ... by using ...` solely to move a method out of subject position.
- **R3:** Follow panel and experiment order unless a different order produces a stronger and still accurate evidence chain. At every change in analytical direction, add the shortest bridge that answers `Why was this analysis performed next?` Retain constructions such as `To determine whether...` when they introduce a genuine subquestion rather than generic scene-setting.
- **R3a — Minimum sufficient bridge:** A bridge should establish direction, not rehearse the full reasoning. State the broad objective in one short clause and allow the comparison groups, method, and result to define the specific alternatives. Stop once the next panel is intelligible.
- **R3b — Positive bridge:** Present the next cohort, model, or analysis as adding validation, resolution, scope, or mechanistic insight. Do not use an earlier dataset's `lack`, `failure`, or `insufficiency` as the stated reason for the next Results analysis. Report necessary limitations separately outside the Results bridge.
- **R4:** Check that direction, group names, units, labels, *n*, error bars, significance marks, and figure callouts agree across figure, legend, and prose.
- **R5:** Compress each paragraph to the minimum complete chain: motivating result → next question when needed → new result → contrast or convergence → inference. Keep routine methods to a short clause and remove repeated summaries, but never remove the premise that makes the next panel intelligible.
- **R6:** Do not insert missing-statistics caveats, generic limitations, or statements about what an experiment did not prove into the revised Results. Put those points in Editorial notes. If a comparison is explicitly nonsignificant, do not call it significant; report the observed direction only when scientifically useful.
- **R7:** Preserve the author's causal or mechanistic framing unless there is a clear logical error. Mandatory downgrades include cross-sectional association presented as causation, colocalization presented as mediation, nonsignificant evidence presented as significant, or an in vitro result presented as clinical efficacy. For borderline cases, retain the author's preferred strength and do not volunteer a flatter alternative unless the user requests claim calibration.
- **R8 — No orphan panels:** Every panel mentioned in Results must have an identifiable purpose within the figure-level argument. A panel is orphaned when its result is reported but the reader cannot tell why that analysis was needed or how it changes the interpretation of the preceding result. Repair orphan panels with a concise question or inference bridge, not with a long methods preamble.
- **R9 — Final continuity test:** Read the Results without relying on panel labels. The reader should be able to reconstruct the sequence `overall question → discovery → unresolved issue → next analysis → integrated conclusion`. If the prose reads as disconnected facts, restore the missing transition before further compression.
- **R10 — No performed thought process:** Do not write the researcher's internal sequence as `the result raised the question whether A, B, or both; we therefore separated A and B`. When the next analysis itself reveals the question, compress this to `To explain the pattern, we classified/separated/tested...`, followed immediately by the result.
- **R11 — Innovation closure:** When a panel group establishes a principal innovation or comparative advantage, end that evidence module with one concise sentence stating the advance explicitly. Numeric overlap or coverage results do not substitute for the conceptual claim that the new marker identifies cases missed by conventional markers.
- **R12 — Separate conclusions for separate evidence modules:** Do not merge distinct claims merely because they occur in one paragraph or figure. For example, `case capture beyond conventional markers` and `association with long-term event risk` are separate findings and may each require their own interpretive sentence.
- **R13 — Explanatory return:** When a later panel provides a plausible explanation for an earlier finding, return explicitly to the earlier result. Use calibrated causal wording such as `may explain` while preserving the author's intended strength. For example, early and persistent separation of survival curves may explain the greater long-term sensitivity of a multivariable protein score than conventional inflammatory markers. This return completes the figure logic and is not redundant.
- **R14 — Connector audit:** Use `thus` and `therefore` only for conclusions directly supported by the preceding result; use `indicate`, `suggest`, `consistently`, `may explain`, or no connector for different logical relationships. Do not distribute connectors merely to create variation or compression.
- **R15 — Conventional wording before compression:** Inspect every compressed phrase for ordinary biomedical meaning, natural collocation, and immediate reader comprehension. Do not coin a shorthand label for an observed comparison when a direct description is clearer. Search representative target-journal or field literature when a specialized or disputed expression affects the prose.
- **R16 — Innovation-forward closure:** State specificity, superiority, persistence, mechanistic importance, or clinical value as strongly as the complete evidence chain permits. Do not make a supported conclusion flat merely to sound cautious; revise only when the design and claim clearly conflict.

## Discussion — D

Required input: ordered main results, intended take-home message, limitations, and either the author's discussion points or permission to propose candidate points.

- **D1:** Before drafting, list a small set of discussion-worthy conclusions derived from the results and ask the user to select when the choice would materially change the narrative. If the direction is already clear, proceed.
- **D2:** First paragraph: prior context and resolved gap → ordered study-level conclusions, with only essential methods → integrated core finding. Mirror the logic of the introduction and results.
- **D3:** For each main point, compare the conclusion with prior literature: supporting or extending evidence, conflicting evidence, differences in species/stage/model, cross-sectional versus temporal evidence, or methodological limitations overcome by the present design.
- **D4:** Build three layers where defensible: relationship to prior evidence → what the present conclusion changes or adds → the clinical or biological problem it may explain. Label the third layer as interpretation when untested.
- **D5:** Use the study's internal convergence across experiments as support, but do not call internal consistency independent validation when the experiments share the same bias or dataset.
- **D6:** Discuss unexpected or conflicting findings directly and offer evidence-based possible explanations. Do not cherry-pick only confirmatory papers.
- **D7:** Translational implications must be proportional to the evidence. Distinguish a candidate target from an effective therapy; include limitations, safety concerns, validation needs, and realistic future studies.
- **D8:** Conclusion paragraph: study + one or two defining approaches if needed → integrated evidence-supported story → specific scientific or clinical relevance. Avoid repeating every result.

## Methods — M

Required input: study design and all unique procedural details. Ask for missing values that would otherwise force invention, including species/strain/sex/age or cell source, intervention identity, concentration, dose, route, timing, sampling time, controls, biological and technical replicates, inclusion/exclusion or blinding, instruments/software/versions, reagent supplier/catalogue, antibody dilution, primer sequence, and ethics approval when relevant.

- **M1:** Be concise but reproducible. Give unique methods enough detail to repeat; summarize established methods as previously described only with a valid reference and any study-specific deviations.
- **M2:** Prefer passive constructions when they improve methods style, and avoid `we`, `our`, and `this study` unless the target journal favors active voice. Clarity outranks a blanket passive-voice rule.
- **M3:** Never fill missing parameters with typical values. Insert an explicit placeholder or question.
- **M4:** Separate biological from technical replicates. State how technical replicates were aggregated and what unit entered statistical analysis.
- **M5:** Place long reagent, antibody, and primer details in a supplementary table when appropriate, while keeping essential identification in the method and following journal requirements.
- **M6:** Check the applicable reporting guideline and ethics requirements for the study design rather than assuming ARRIVE or another framework applies.

## Figure Legends — F

Required input per turn: one figure with all panels, conditions, sample/replicate information, measured endpoints, methods, labels/symbols, scale bars, statistics, and target-journal instructions.

- **F1:** Write a compact caption, not a mini-Results or mini-Methods section. Describe what each panel shows and only the experimental detail needed to interpret it.
- **F2:** Give the figure a concise, result-oriented title that is distinct from the corresponding Results subsection heading. Use the strongest supported finding, including specificity, superiority, persistence, or mechanism when established, rather than a generic topic label. Do not copy the Results title verbatim. Based on the supplied reference style, use roughly 8–16 words for the title when no journal rule is available.
- **F3:** Describe panels in order and group related panels, for example `(A–C)` or `(F,G)`, when they share an experiment. Prefer one compact sentence per panel or grouped panel. State the assay, comparison, endpoint, and symbol meanings; omit procedural chronology already clear from the schematic or Methods.
- **F4:** Define `n` and state whether it represents independent animals, participants, cell preparations, experiments, or another biological unit. State technical replicates separately.
- **F5:** Name the summary measure and error bars exactly as analyzed (for example mean ± SD, mean ± SEM, or median with interquartile range). Never prefer SEM merely for journal prestige.
- **F6:** State the statistical test, sidedness when relevant, multiple-comparison procedure, comparison represented by each symbol, and exact or threshold *P* values according to journal style. Include test statistics and degrees of freedom only when required or available.
- **F7:** For immunoblots, report relevant molecular-mass markers, loading control, and quantification basis when available. For histology or imaging, report stain/channel identity, magnification if useful, and scale bar. Do not demand details that belong exclusively in Methods under the target journal's policy.
- **F7a — Mandatory scale-bar gate:** Inspect every supplied image for visible scale bars. If any panel contains a scale bar, obtain the exact physical length and its panel-specific mapping from the user before writing the final legend. If several panels use different lengths, request each mapping separately. Do not estimate a length from pixels, assume that identical-looking bars share a value, silently omit scale-bar information, use `as indicated`, or finalize the legend with a placeholder. Pause the figure-legend task until the user supplies or explicitly confirms the required value(s).
- **F8:** Verify legend–figure agreement: every panel, group, color, symbol, abbreviation, scale bar, and significance mark must be accounted for, and nothing may be described that is absent from the figure.
- **F9:** Check the target journal's current length and formatting rules. If no explicit rule is available, target 150–250 words for a complete multi-panel legend and treat approximately 300 words as a soft ceiling for unusually complex figures. A short omics figure may require only 100–180 words. These are house defaults inferred from the supplied examples, not universal journal limits.
- **F10:** Put sample size near the relevant panel and consolidate scale bars, abbreviations, data presentation, statistical tests, and significance symbols at the end when this shortens the legend.
- **F11:** Use neutral panel descriptions such as `Representative images`, `Quantification`, or `Volcano plots showing...`. Reserve interpretation for the title and only the minimum wording needed to explain a non-obvious comparison.

### Reference-style pattern

The supplied examples share the following pattern: a 10–14-word declarative title; panels described sequentially with related panels grouped; representative images distinguished from quantification; biological `n` and analyzed fields stated explicitly; scale bars, abbreviations, and significance thresholds consolidated at the end; and little repetition of the Results narrative. Reproduce this economy and organization without copying source wording.
