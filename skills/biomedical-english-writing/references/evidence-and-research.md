# Evidence and Research

Use current web research whenever the task depends on literature, terminology, nomenclature, reporting guidance, or journal instructions. Cite direct links near the relevant claim in the response.

## Build a Working Knowledge Base

For a new manuscript workflow, assemble a compact, revisable knowledge base before substantial drafting:

1. research question, disease/system, species or population, and study design;
2. target journal, article type, current author instructions, word/figure limits, and style constraints;
3. key terms and official nomenclature;
4. established findings, active controversies, and the precise unresolved question;
5. the study's ordered direct results, conclusions, and proposed extensions;
6. a claim-to-source map for introduction and discussion statements.

Show the user a concise synthesis rather than dumping search results. Record uncertainty and conflicts instead of forcing consensus.

## Source Priority

Prefer sources in this order, adjusted to the claim:

1. official journal author instructions and official nomenclature/reporting sites;
2. original peer-reviewed studies for specific experimental or clinical findings;
3. current consensus statements, guidelines, systematic reviews, or authoritative reviews for broad context;
4. high-quality databases for identifiers and standardized names.

Search results, snippets, generic health sites, and AI-generated summaries are discovery aids, not evidence. Verify DOI, title, authorship, year, population/species, design, and the exact supported claim. Never invent a citation or recommend a paper based only on its title.

## Claim-to-Source Check

For each sentence needing support:

- isolate the smallest factual claim;
- decide whether it needs a primary study, review, guideline, database, or journal policy;
- check that the source matches the species, disease context, intervention, outcome, and direction of effect;
- note whether the source supports, qualifies, or conflicts with the claim;
- recommend no more than three sources, prioritizing the closest evidence.

If no suitable source is found, mark `[reference needed—no verified source found]` and weaken or remove the claim. Do not use a related but non-supporting citation.

## Terminology and Nomenclature Checks

Use official sources when applicable, including:

- HGNC for approved human gene symbols and names;
- MGI for mouse gene nomenclature;
- NCBI Gene, UniProt, MeSH, ICD, or another domain authority for identifiers and preferred terminology;
- the appropriate EQUATOR Network reporting guideline for study design;
- the target journal's current author instructions for house style.

Confirm whether the passage refers to a gene, transcript, protein, cell population, assay, drug, or clinical entity. Report the verified form and source link in the evidence notes.

### Phrase and Collocation Verification

Verify a phrase when it is specialized, unusually compressed, disputed by the author, or being used to carry a central conclusion. The purpose is to secure natural, precise wording without flattening the scientific claim.

1. Search for the exact phrase and close variants in representative papers from the target journal or field.
2. Inspect the full sentence, not only the search snippet, and confirm that the phrase performs the same semantic function in a comparable scientific context.
3. Prefer several independent, well-edited peer-reviewed examples before treating a compressed expression as established. A single occurrence proves only that the phrase has been used, not that it fits the current sentence.
4. If a phrase is rare, opaque, or used differently in the literature, replace it with a direct description using familiar biomedical vocabulary. Do not invent a new label merely to save words.
5. Do not search every ordinary word or transparent construction. Concentrate verification on nomenclature, technical terms, statistical meanings, field-specific collocations, disputed expressions, and wording that materially affects interpretation.
6. Keep verification citations in the response or Editorial notes; do not insert citations into the authors' Results merely to justify ordinary wording.

Distinguish conventionality from contextual necessity. Expressions such as `at the cohort level`, `clinical onset`, or `span multiple pathways` may occur in published biomedical writing yet remain unnecessary or too abstract in a particular sentence. Retain them only when they make a real analytical, clinical, or mechanistic distinction.

## Journal Calibration

Use target papers to learn scope, rhetorical density, section organization, field terminology, and the acceptable strength of titles and take-home claims, but do not copy their sentences. Separate journal requirements from stylistic observations. If the journal or article type is not known, provide a journal-neutral version that retains the strongest defensible scientific conclusion rather than defaulting to conservative wording.

## Literature Use by Section

- **Introduction:** establish context and a fair, defensible gap; include evidence that limits the novelty claim.
- **Discussion:** seek both concordant and discordant studies, prioritizing comparable species, disease stage, population, and design; explain why differences may matter.
- **Methods:** use references for genuinely established protocols and state study-specific modifications.
- **Results and figure legends:** citations are uncommon and should not substitute for missing experimental detail.
- **Abstract:** normally omit citations, but verify factual statements internally.
